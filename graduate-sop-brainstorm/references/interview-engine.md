# Adaptive Interview Engine

Ask to repair the missing layer, not to complete a script. At most 3–5 questions in a round. After the answer, re-diagnose. Do not continue the previous question type if the material has moved.

The student does not have to answer in chronological order. If they say the same problem appeared in a later internship, jump there. That jump is often the recurring pattern.

## Classify the material first

| Type | What you have | Ask next |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A. Facts only | "I researched X in a lab." | Action and Decision |
| B. Action, no thought | They can narrate the work and not why it is worth writing | Decision and Tension |
| C. Action and result, no change | Strong CV episode, no shift in understanding | Reflection |
| D. Abstract reflection | "This made me realize technology matters." | Concrete evidence, scene, and the exact turn |
| E. Many experiences, no line | Projects that do not speak to each other | Pattern or recurring question |
| F. Motive without evidence | They want a field the file does not yet explain | Search backward from the motive into past episodes |

## Six-layer depth map

Use this to choose the layer, not to fill all six by reflex.

| Layer | Question |
| --- | --- |
| Fact | What happened? |
| Action | What did the student do? |
| Decision | Why that choice? |
| Tension | Where was the problem or contradiction? |
| Reflection | How did their understanding change? |
| Implication | Why does this lead to the target field? |

Ask the missing layer whose answer would most deepen the essay.

## Decision trees

### 1. No concrete scene

They say "the model did not perform well." Do not ask what they learned. Ask which test, experiment, or task showed it; what they actually saw; what they did first.

### 2. Scene, but the stakes are unclear

Ask what made them feel this was not an ordinary bug, and whether they would still be in this field if that problem had never appeared.

### 3. Action that does not show thinking

Ask which options they had, why they chose this one, and whether a judgment was forced.

### 4. A result with no depth

Do not write "this experience taught me." Ask where the result differed from what they expected, what they had believed, and which fact forced the belief to change.

### 5. Reflection that is too broad

"I realized AI needs to be more reliable" is not yet a thought. Ask what "reliable" meant there, in which scene, what the failure did, and whether the same problem returned later.

### 6. A turning point appears

The moment they say "I used to think… then I found…" stop ordinary questions and do a turning-point deep dive:

1. What was the old view?
2. Why did they hold it?
3. Which fact changed the judgment?
4. What is the new understanding?
5. Did that new understanding change a later choice?

## Question craft

- One scene, one decision, or one contradiction per question.
- Use their nouns. If they said "not robust," ask what failed, not "what is robustness in the literature."
- Do not ask "what did you learn?" while the scene is still vague.
- Do not offer interpretations for them to agree with. Ask for the fact, then let them name the change.
- If they answer with a slogan, quote the slogan and ask for the case.
