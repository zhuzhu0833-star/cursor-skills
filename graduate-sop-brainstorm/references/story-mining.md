# Story Mining

## Hidden turning point

The best essay often does not come from the most dramatic episode. Look for the small event that later changed direction: a failed experiment, an anomalous model result, a comment from an advisor, one concept in a course, a user's complaint, a project conflict, a real-world case, a problem they had been ignoring. Rank cognitive consequence above event size.

## Recurring pattern

When there are several experiences, do not process them one by one and then stack them. Ask whether they are the same problem in different clothes.

Example: a model that is accurate but unexplainable; a deployed system that breaks in use; a theory that will not fit the data. The pattern is the gap between a technical solution and a real environment. That pattern becomes the central question, which is more graduate-level than "I did three AI projects."

Useful follow-ups: is it the same problem in both places; how did the understanding differ the second time; which episode actually changed direction.

## Contradiction

Do not rush to make two episodes consistent. A project that worships efficiency, and a user study where efficiency is not usability. Accuracy up, decision value flat. Theory that works, a deployment that does not. Turn the clash into tension, then into a question.

## Narrative value

The experience to write is the one that best explains how this person became who they are now. Prestige is not the test. Prefer the episode that has a scene, a personal action, a decision, a conflict, a change in understanding, a link to the field, and a voice that could not be swapped onto another applicant. A plain internship can beat a famous contest. A failed experiment can beat a prize. One observation can beat a list of awards.

## Story candidates

Generate 3–5. Each one needs all of the following. If a field is empty, the candidate is not ready to draft.

- **Story title.** One sentence.
- **Scene.** What concretely happened.
- **Tension.** Where the problem was.
- **Turning point.** The moment understanding changed.
- **Anchor.** What the story is really about.
- **Academic question.** The question it produces.
- **Why major.** Why this field is required.
- **Why this student.** Why only this person can tell it.

Discard a candidate that is impressive and empty, even if the student is proud of it. Recommend the candidate with the strongest causal path from scene to graduate need, and say what the others are missing.
